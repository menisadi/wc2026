How the Model Works

A high-level tour of the pipeline: from raw match data, through Elo and a Poisson scoring model, to a Monte-Carlo simulation of the entire 48-team tournament.

Overview Poisson model Elo ratings

The big picture

The engine answers questions like “who wins this match?” and “who lifts the trophy?” by combining two complementary models. Elo provides a global, self-correcting strength score for every nation. The Poisson model turns strengths into expected goals and a full distribution of scorelines. A Monte-Carlo simulator then plays the whole tournament tens of thousands of times to turn single-match probabilities into trophy odds.

🎯 Elo — strength

One rating per team, updated after every historical match. The shared yardstick that lets the engine compare teams that never meet. Read more →

⚽ Poisson — goals

Learned attack/defense + Elo features → expected goals → win/draw/loss and scoreline probabilities. Read more →

The pipeline, end to end

Every command (predict-match, simulate, snapshot…) runs the same load-and-train path before doing its specific job:

1

Load data

Historical international results (2010+), the 2026 schedule, and FIFA rankings are loaded. Team names are normalized to a single canonical form so nothing silently mismatches.

load_results · load_schedule · load_rankings
2

Compute Elo history

Replay all matches chronologically to build a per-year rating for every team (all 321), cached to disk. This is the global strength signal.

load_or_compute_elo_history
3

Build team strengths

For the 48 World Cup teams, assemble the latest Elo + ranking features used at prediction time.

build_team_strengths
4

Train the Poisson model

Fit attack/defense coefficients (+ Elo strength features) on recency-weighted history. The result can produce xG for any pairing.

PoissonModel.fit
5

Extract groups & simulate

The 12 groups are recovered from the schedule, then the tournament is played out thousands of times. Already-played 2026 results are injected as fixed outcomes.

extract_groups · run_monte_carlo
Point-in-time correctness. For historical snapshots, both the training data and the Elo history are frozen to a cutoff_date, so each day's probabilities reflect only what was known then — no leakage from future results.

Simulating the tournament

One simulated tournament threads many single-match predictions through the real competition format:

Group stage12 groups × 4 teams · round-robin, 3/1/0 points
Qualify12 winners + 12 runners-up + 8 best third-place = 32
Round of 32winners cross-paired vs runners-up; thirds paired together
R16 → QF → SFsingle-elimination knockouts (draws → penalties, 50/50)
Finallast team standing = champion of this run

Each match outcome comes straight from the Poisson model: group games sample a scoreline, knockout games sample until there's a winner. Run this N times (default 10,000–50,000) and count how often each team reaches each stage — that frequency is the probability.

Three scenario modes

Is it any good? (backtesting)

The model isn't taken on faith. backtest does a walk-forward evaluation: train only on the past, predict each subsequent year's matches, and score the probabilities with proper metrics (log-loss, Brier, ranked probability score, accuracy). It competes against baselines — uniform, home-win, elo-only, a random Poisson, and others — so any claimed skill is measured, not assumed. A calibration table checks that, e.g., outcomes predicted at 30% actually happen ~30% of the time.
